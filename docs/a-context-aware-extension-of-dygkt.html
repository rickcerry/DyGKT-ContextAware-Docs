A Context-Aware Extension of DyGKT

Introduction to the problem

As explained in the previous section, DyGKT constructs local representations of students and questions by retrieving the most recent N interactions occurring before the target timestamp. Therefore, recency is the main criterion determining which historical interactions are provided to the model.

This mechanism guar\antees temporal consistency and limits the computational complexity. However, the most recent interactions are not necessarily the most informative ones for predicting the target student-question interaction. Since only a fixed number N of historical interactions can be provided to the model, selecting one interaction means excluding another one that could potentially contain more useful information.

For example, on the student side, older interactions involving the same knowledge concept as the target question may provide more relevant information about the student’s current knowledge than more recent interactions involving unrelated concepts. Similarly, on the question side, interactions from students showing a learning behavior similar to the target student may be more informative for characterizing the question than interactions from arbitrary recent students.

Therefore, temporal proximity and contextual relevance do not necessarily coincide. Recency remains important, since a student’s knowledge evolves over time, but it does not necessarily need to be the only criterion determining which interactions are included in the local neighborhood.

This motivates the investigation of more context-aware neighborhood selection strategies for DyGKT. Instead of modifying the downstream architecture, the idea is to improve the historical context provided to the model by selecting interactions based not only on their temporal proximity, but also on their relevance to the target prediction, while preserving temporal consistency and the same fixed neighbor budget.

The leads to the main research question investigated in this work:

Can DyGKT’s predictive performance be improved by incorporating contextual relevance into neighborhood selection while preserving temporal consistency?

Formalisation of the problem

This work has primarily focused on the student representation, where the objective is to select historical question interactions by balancing temporal proximity and contextual relevance.

A central aspect of this approach is therefore defining what constitutes the context of an interaction. In this work, context is represented by the skill associated with each question.

Consequently, interactions involving questions associated with the same skill as the target question are considered more contextually relevant, while temporal information is retained to account for the evolution of the student’s knowledge over time.

The proposed sampling strategy therefore aims to balance these two dimensions, rather than relying exclusively on recency as in the original DyGKT formulation.

The refined research question is:

Can the predictive performance of DyGKT be improved by selecting student-side historical interactions based on a balance between temporal proximity and skill-based contextual relevance?

Motivation from the data

To investigate whether the recency-based neighborhood construction of DyGKT may exclude potentially relevant historical information, a preliminary analysis was conducted on the ASSIST17 dataset, focusing on student-side interaction histories. For each target interaction, the N=50 most recent interactions of the same student occurring strictly before the target timestamp were retrieved, following the original neighborhood-selection principle of DyGKT. The selected interactions were then compared with the target question in terms of their associated skill.

Moreover, the median is only 5 same-skill interactions out of 50, indicating that for at least half of these target interactions, no more than 10% of the selected neighborhood is directly associated with the target skill.

This observation does not imply that same-skill interactions are necessarily more informative than recent interactions. However, it highlights an important property of the neighborhood provided to DyGKT: temporal proximity does not necessarily correspond to skill-based contextual relevance. Since the model is restricted to a fixed neighborhood of N=50 interactions, recent interactions associated with other skills may occupy most of the available context, while older interactions related to the skill currently being assessed are excluded.

This provides the empirical motivation for investigating a context-aware neighbor-selection strategy. Rather than replacing temporal information, the proposed approach investigates whether predictive performance can be improved by balancing temporal proximity with skill-based contextual relevance, allowing the fixed neighbor budget to include historical interactions that may be more closely related to the target prediction.

Implementation

Algorithm

The proposed neighborhood-selection strategy is summarized in the algorithm above.

Given a target interaction e=(q,s,k,t), the procedure first retrieves the most recent γ N interactions of student s occurring strictly before the target timestamp t. This set defines a larger candidate pool from which the final N neighbors are selected.

Each candidate interaction ei​ is assigned a score based on its temporal rank Ri​ within the candidate set and on the relationship between its associated skill ki​ and the target skill k. The candidates are then ranked according to this score, and the N highest-scoring interactions are retained. Finally, the selected interactions are reordered chronologically before being provided to DyGKT.

In this way, the neighborhood size remains fixed at N, as in the original DyGKT formulation, while the model is allowed to select historical interactions from a broader temporal window according to both recency and contextual relevance.

Experiments

Data management and preprocessing

Important note. The original DyGKT paper does not provide a complete description of the preprocessing pipeline, and some of the required preprocessing choices could not be directly inferred from the reported methodology. Therefore, defining a preprocessing procedure specifically for this work was necessary. The resulting pipeline presents some differences from the one presumably used in the original experiments; these differences and the corresponding assumptions will be explicitly highlighted in the relevant preprocessing steps below.

The original interaction data were processed to obtain a temporal bipartite graph representation compatible with DyGKT.

First, the relevant dataset fields were standardized into a common schema containing student, question, timestamp, skill, and correctness information. Interaction timestamps were converted to Unix time in seconds, while interactions without an associated skill were retained by assigning them to a dedicated noskill category.

The noskill category was assigned ID 1 rather than 0, since ID 0 is reserved for padding. This distinction is particularly relevant for the proposed contextual sampling strategy, where skill information is used to compute the relevance score. Padding entries must remain distinguishable from actual interactions for which no skill information is available. For interactions associated with noskill, the skill-based contribution to the score is therefore set to zero.

As done in the original configuration of DyGKT, to reduce the presence of sparsely represented nodes, students and questions with fewer than five interactions were iteratively removed. The filtering procedure was repeated until all remaining students and questions satisfied the minimum-interaction requirement. The resulting interactions were then sorted chronologically to preserve the temporal structure required by the dynamic graph model.

The preprocessed ASSIST17 dataset provided in the authors’ GitHub repository splits students with long interaction histories into multiple student nodes, such that each resulting student node contains at most 100 interactions. Consequently, a single real student may be represented by multiple student IDs. This batching procedure was not applied in the preprocessing pipeline used in this work, so that each original student is represented by a single student node and their complete interaction history is preserved.

Student, question, and skill identifiers were subsequently mapped to consecutive integer IDs. Each interaction was represented as a timestamped edge between a student and a question node, with answer correctness encoded as a binary edge feature. Student and question identifiers were assigned to separate ID ranges to obtain a bipartite graph representation, with index 0 reserved for padding. Finally, the skill associated with each question was stored as a question-node feature.

In accordance with the DyGKT formulation, each question node was associated with a single skill, even when multiple skill annotations were available across its interactions. Since the paper does not explicitly describe how this single skill is selected, the procedure was inferred by inspecting the original implementation. This analysis indicated that the skill assigned to each question corresponds to the most recent skill associated with that question in the interaction history. The same procedure was therefore adopted during preprocessing to remain consistent with the original DyGKT experimental setup.

The resulting processed data consist of a temporal edge list, edge features encoding answer correctness, and node features encoding question skills, which are then used as input to DyGKT.

The complete data preprocessing pipeline is provided as a Jupyter Notebook in the accompanying GitHub repository.

Evaluation setup

The evaluation procedure followed the same experimental setup adopted in the original DyGKT implementation.

Model performance was evaluated using Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) and Average Precision (AP), which are the two metrics reported in the original DyGKT paper. Results were computed over three independent runs, using different random seeds, and are reported as mean ± standard deviation.

Before evaluating the proposed contextual neighbor-selection strategy, the original DyGKT model was reproduced and used as the main baseline. The hyperparameters of the baseline were initially set according to the configuration reported by the authors. Additional experiments were then conducted on the parameters directly related to neighborhood construction, particularly the number of historical neighbors N and the size of the candidate historical window used by the proposed sampler. The same evaluation protocol and data splits were maintained across configurations to ensure a fair comparison.

During the reproduction experiments, the obtained results did not match those reported in the original DyGKT paper, despite using the provided implementation and following the reported experimental configuration.

For this reason, the evaluation of the proposed method does not rely on a direct comparison with the numerical results reported in the paper. Instead, all comparisons are made against the locally reproduced DyGKT baseline, obtained under the same computational environment, preprocessing pipeline, data splits, and evaluation procedure used for the proposed extension.

All experiments were conducted on the Berzelius supercomputer at the National Supercomputer Centre (NSC), Sweden. Each experiment was allocated a single NVIDIA A100-SXM4 GPU with 80 GB of memory, together with 16 CPU cores. The experiments were run using Python 3.13.14, PyTorch 2.13.0, and CUDA 13.0. The same computational environment and resource configuration were used for both the reproduced DyGKT baseline and the proposed contextual extension.

The experimental results show a relatively consistent effect of the neighborhood size N across the two datasets, whereas the effect of the candidate-window parameter γ appears to be dataset-dependent.

On ASSIST17, the best results across all four evaluation metrics are obtained with N=100 and γ=10. In particular, this configuration achieves a Test AP of 0.7333 and a Test ROC-AUC of 0.8138, compared with 0.7294 and 0.8113, respectively, when γ=1. A similar improvement is observed in the new-node evaluation. This suggests that, for ASSIST17, considering a larger candidate pool before selecting the final N historical interactions can provide a small but consistent benefit over relying exclusively on the most recent interactions.

A different behavior is observed on ASSIST12. While larger values of N generally lead to better performance, the best results are obtained with N=100 and γ=1. Increasing γ does not provide a consistent improvement, and the differences between the tested values are generally small. Therefore, on this dataset, expanding the candidate pool and re-ranking historical interactions according to contextual relevance does not appear to provide an advantage over the original recency-based selection.

Overall, these results suggest that contextual neighborhood selection is not universally beneficial. Its effectiveness depends on the characteristics of the interaction data: while ASSIST17 shows a consistent improvement when contextual selection is applied to a larger historical candidate set, the same behavior is not observed on ASSIST12. At the same time, the results across both datasets indicate that the number of historical interactions provided to the model plays an important role, with N=100 consistently producing the strongest overall performance among the evaluated neighborhood sizes.
