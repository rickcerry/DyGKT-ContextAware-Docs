Conclusions

Discussion of the results

The results obtained on ASSIST17 provide an interesting perspective on the effectiveness of the proposed neighbor-selection strategy. Although the improvement in the best overall predictive performance is relatively small, this is not the most relevant outcome of the experiments. A more important result can be observed when considering smaller neighborhood sizes.

For lower values of N, the proposed approach generally achieves better predictive performance compared with the original recency-based sampling strategy. At the same time, the variability across different trials is reduced, leading to more stable results. This suggests that selecting historical interactions based not only on recency, but also on their contextual relevance, can allow the model to construct a more informative neighborhood even when fewer interactions are provided.

This result is particularly relevant from a computational perspective. If comparable performance can be obtained using a smaller neighborhood, the model needs to process fewer historical interactions for each prediction. Therefore, a more selective neighborhood construction may provide a way to reduce the amount of information processed by the model without sacrificing predictive performance. In this sense, the proposed approach suggests that improving which interactions are selected can be more beneficial than simply increasing the number of interactions available to the model.

Another important observation concerns the choice of γ, which determines the size of the candidate pool from which the final N interactions are selected. The experimental results suggest that the effectiveness of this parameter is dataset-dependent, as the same value of γ does not provide the same benefit across the considered datasets.

This is reasonable considering that different datasets may present different interaction patterns, sequence lengths, and distributions of skills across students. As a consequence, expanding the candidate pool may be beneficial when relevant interactions are more sparsely distributed in the student’s history, while providing limited additional information when useful interactions are already concentrated among the most recent ones.

Therefore, γ should not necessarily be interpreted as a fixed parameter of the proposed sampling strategy, but rather as a hyperparameter that should be selected according to the characteristics of the dataset.

Limitations and future directions

Collaborative filtering

During the internship, an extension of the contextual sampling strategy based on collaborative filtering was also explored. While the approach presented in this work focuses on the student-side neighborhood, where historical interactions are selected according to their temporal proximity and skill relevance, a similar idea was investigated for the question-side neighborhood.

A similar approach has been explored in previous work, where collaborative information was incorporated into a Knowledge Tracing model. Please refer to the References section for further details.

In this case, contextual relevance cannot be directly defined through skill similarity in the same way. Instead, the idea was to estimate the similarity between students based on their previous interactions with questions. For each student, a behavior representation was constructed from the most recent available outcome on previously attempted questions. Correct and incorrect answers were represented with different values, while questions for which no previous interaction was available did not contribute to the comparison. The similarity between two students could then be estimated from these representations, providing a collaborative signal for determining which historical question-side interactions might be more relevant to the target student.

The resulting score therefore provides a simple measure of behavioral agreement between students based on their shared interaction history.

The normalization prevents the similarity score from being influenced by the number of questions attempted by each student, making the comparison more representative of their behavioral agreement.

However, computing and maintaining these student representations, and evaluating similarities between students during neighborhood construction, introduced a considerably higher computational cost compared with the skill-based student-side strategy. This became particularly relevant for large interaction datasets and made extensive experimentation with the approach impractical within the available computational and time constraints.

Unlike skill information, student behavior representations cannot be computed once and statically stored. Since DyGKT operates on a dynamic graph, each behavior vector bst​ must reflect only the interactions available before the target timestamp t. As new interactions occur, these vectors need to be updated, and consequently student similarities may change over time. Maintaining these dynamic representations and repeatedly computing similarities therefore introduces a substantial additional computational cost.

For this reason, the final evaluation focused on the student-side contextual sampler.

Nevertheless, the collaborative filtering approach represents a natural direction for extending the proposed method. A more computationally efficient implementation of student similarity could make it possible to incorporate contextual relevance on both sides of the bipartite graph, rather than modifying only the student-side neighborhood construction.

Multi-skill interactions

Another limitation of the current implementation concerns questions associated with multiple skills. The proposed contextual sampling strategy uses skill information to determine which historical interactions are more relevant to the target interaction. However, in the current implementation, each question is represented by a single skill.

This assumption directly affects the neighborhood construction. When a question is associated with multiple skills, considering only one of them provides an incomplete representation of its knowledge requirements. As a consequence, a historical interaction may be considered less relevant simply because its associated skill does not match the single skill selected for the target question, even though the two questions may actually share another skill. Similarly, the relevance of some interactions may not be represented correctly when constructing the final neighborhood.

A natural extension would therefore be to associate each question with its complete set of skills and extend the contextual scoring function to account for the full set of skills, as originally intended.

Other future directions

The experimental analysis presented in this work was conducted by keeping ϵ fixed. This choice was based on preliminary experiments in which different values of ϵ were tested while keeping N constant and repeating the evaluation across different trials and sampling configurations. Within the explored range, changing ϵ did not appear to substantially affect the behavior of the model. For this reason, ϵ was fixed in the subsequent experiments, allowing the analysis to focus primarily on the effects of N and γ.

However, the experiments conducted during the internship were not intended to provide an exhaustive hyperparameter analysis. A more systematic study of ϵ, potentially considering a wider range of values and its interaction with N and γ, could provide a better understanding of how the balance between temporal proximity and contextual relevance affects neighborhood construction.

The evaluation could also be extended to additional Knowledge Tracing datasets. This would be particularly relevant for studying γ, as the results obtained in this work suggest that the effect of the candidate-pool size may depend on the characteristics of the dataset. Evaluating a wider range of values across datasets with different interaction and skill distributions could therefore help determine when expanding the candidate neighborhood is beneficial.

Finally, future work could investigate alternative scoring functions. The scoring function proposed in this work represents one possible way of combining temporal and contextual information, but different formulations could provide a more effective balance between these components. Comparing different scoring strategies would help determine whether the observed benefits originate from the specific formulation adopted here or, more generally, from incorporating contextual relevance into the neighbor-selection process.

Scope and practical constraints

The scope of the work conducted during the internship was relatively focused. A significant part of the initial phase was dedicated to understanding the DyGKT architecture and its implementation in sufficient detail to identify how the neighborhood-selection mechanism could be modified.

In particular, this required understanding the data preprocessing pipeline, the construction of the temporal graph, and how student interactions are stored and processed during training and evaluation. These aspects were especially important since the proposed modification directly operates on the historical interactions provided to the model. Therefore, modifying the sampling procedure required a clear understanding of how these interactions are stored, retrieved, and passed through the different stages of the pipeline.

Additional work was dedicated to data cleaning and preprocessing in order to obtain a representation compatible with DyGKT while preserving the temporal and skill information required by the proposed sampler.

For these reasons, the internship focused on a relatively specific modification of the original model rather than exploring a broader range of alternative approaches. However, this work was necessary to properly implement and evaluate the proposed extension within the original DyGKT framework, while also providing a foundation for the possible future extensions discussed above.
