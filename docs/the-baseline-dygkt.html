The Baseline: DyGKT

Introduction to the model

Most Knowledge Tracing approaches model student learning as a sequence of question-answer interactions. Given the previous interactions of a student, the objective is to estimate the student’s current knowledge state and predict whether the student will correctly answer a future question. Although sequential approaches have achieved strong predictive performance, they provide a relatively static representation of a learning process that is inherently dynamic.

Cheng et al. introduced Dynamic Graph-based Knowledge Tracing (DyGKT), which reformulates Knowledge Tracing as a continuous-time dynamic graph learning problem. Instead of independently representing each student’s history as a sequence, DyGKT considers the complete set of student-question interactions as an evolving graph. This formulation is motivated by three main characteristics of educational interaction data: its continuously growing scale, the irregular temporal intervals between interactions, and the evolving relationships among students, questions, and knowledge concepts.

Dynamic Graph formulation

DyGKT represents educational interactions as a continuous-time dynamic graph (CTDG). It considers two types of nodes: student nodes and question nodes, forming a bipartite graph.

Whenever a student answers a question, an interaction is created between the corresponding student and question nodes.

Under this formulation, Knowledge Tracing becomes a dynamic link classification problem. Given a student s, a question q, and a timestamp t, the model predicts the label of the interaction between the two nodes, corresponding to whether the student will correctly answer the question.

This formulation differs substantially from conventional sequence-based KT. Rather than constructing a fixed interaction sequence for each student and predicting its next element, the complete educational dataset is represented as a stream of timestamped interactions. The graph consequently evolves whenever a new student-question interaction occurs.

DyGKT does not process the entire historical neighborhood. Instead, it samples the N most recent historical interactions for each side of the target edge. Therefore, the student representation is constructed from the student’s recent question-answering history, while the question representation is constructed from the recent interactions of other students with that question.

This dual perspective represents an important difference from conventional KT methods. The prediction is not based exclusively on what the target student has previously done. The model simultaneously estimates the evolving knowledge state of the student and the evolving difficulty of the question from their respective temporal neighborhoods.

If fewer than N historical interactions are available, the sequences are padded to obtain a fixed length, allowing efficient batched computation.

Student and question embeddings (E unit)

For each selected historical interaction, DyGKT constructs an interaction representation that combines multiple sources of information describing the interaction, including

  • Observed performance (correct or incorrect response)
  • Temporal information (time elapsed between interactions)
  • Structural information derived from the interaction history (historical student–question interaction patterns)

Two separate sequences of interaction representations are constructed from the two perspectives of the bipartite graph:

  • From the student perspective, the sequence represents the historical questions attempted by the target student s.
  • From the question perspective, it represents the historical students who previously attempted the target question q.

The resulting interaction representations are then arranged in temporal order to form two encoded interaction sequences. These sequences are processed independently by two GRU-based sequential encoders. Consequently, the interaction encoding stage produces two time-aware representations:

  • A student-side encoding hst​, summarizing the historical learning behavior of student s;
  • A question-side encoding hqt​, capturing the dynamic historical context associated with question q up to timestamp t.

These two representations are subsequently combined and used to predict the outcome of the target student–question interaction.

Performance prediction (P unit)

The final prediction combines the dynamically constructed student and question representations. Their embeddings are passed through a prediction unit and the resulting value represents the probability that student s will correctly answer question q at time t.

DyGKT as a starting point

DyGKT was selected as the reference model for this work for several reasons.

First, it is one of the more recent graph-based approaches to Knowledge Tracing and provides an intuitive formulation of the problem based on retrieving and encoding relevant historical neighbors for each target interaction. This formulation naturally incorporates different sources of information, including temporal and structural interaction patterns.

Second, the main contribution of DyGKT is not simply the application of another neural architecture to Knowledge Tracing, but rather a different formulation of the KT problem. While traditional approaches primarily model the learning history of each student as an independent sequence, DyGKT represents educational activity as an evolving bipartite interaction graph. This allows predictions to exploit both the student’s previous interactions and the historical interactions associated with the target question.

Another important aspect is computational complexity. Rather than processing the entire interaction graph for every prediction, DyGKT operates on a limited number of sampled historical neighbors. This makes neighbor selection an explicit and computationally manageable component of the model, while also providing a natural point at which different sampling strategies can be introduced and studied.

Lastly, DyGKT achieves competitive performance on established Knowledge Tracing benchmarks, demonstrating that the dynamic-graph formulation can effectively capture information relevant to predicting future student performance.

These characteristics make DyGKT particularly suitable for this work, providing a strong reference model on which to investigate potential improvements and propose modifications to its existing formulation.
